## It doesn't always look like panic

When people picture anxiety, they often picture a panic attack: a racing heart, short breath, the urge to leave the room. That's real, and it's one way anxiety shows up. But many of the people I see have never had one. Their anxiety is quieter. It hides inside habits that look like personality, or like being busy, or like being tired.

Here are five quiet signs I see often. None of them on its own means you have an anxiety disorder. But if several sound familiar, they're worth paying attention to.

## You plan your life around avoiding something

You take the long way to skip the highway. You let calls go to voicemail and reply by text. You've stopped going to something you used to enjoy because of what might happen there.

Avoidance works in the short term, which is why it's so convincing: the anxiety drops the moment you step away. The cost is that your world quietly gets smaller, and the next time feels harder.

## Your body is keeping score

Anxiety lives in the body as much as the mind. A jaw that aches in the morning. Shoulders that sit up near your ears. A stomach that turns before every meeting. Trouble falling asleep, or waking at 4 a.m. with your mind already running.

If a doctor has ruled out other causes and these keep coming back, it's worth asking whether worry is part of the picture.

## "What if" has become a loop

Everyone worries. Anxiety is when worry stops solving problems and starts running laps. What if I said the wrong thing? What if the results come back bad? What if they're upset with me?

One useful question: is this worry leading me toward something I can actually do? If the answer is usually no, and the loop runs for hours, that's a sign.

## You're irritable and tired, and can't say why

Being on alert all day is exhausting. Many people with anxiety describe themselves as short-tempered or burned out rather than worried. They snap at people they love, then feel guilty, then worry about that too.

If you're always tired but not doing much more than usual, it may be the effort of staying on guard that's wearing you down.

## You need reassurance, or you put things off

Checking that the door is locked, then checking again. Asking a partner whether they're sure they're not angry. Rereading an email ten times before sending it, or not sending it at all.

Reassurance and procrastination look like opposites, but they often come from the same place: trying to make an uncomfortable feeling go away. Both bring relief for a moment, and both tend to make anxiety stronger over time.

## What helps

The good news is that anxiety is one of the most treatable things people bring to therapy. Approaches like cognitive behavioral therapy have strong evidence behind them, and many people notice a difference within a few months.

A few things can help in the meantime:

- Notice one thing you've been avoiding, and take one small step toward it.
- Give worry a set time, ten or fifteen minutes a day, and postpone it outside that window.
- Protect sleep, movement and regular meals, the plain basics anxiety wears down first.
- Tell one person you trust how you've really been.

> Anxiety is a signal, not a sentence. It can be turned down.
