## Two good roads

Open a few provider profiles and you'll see the same words come up under approach: CBT, psychodynamic, sometimes both. If you're new to therapy, it's fair to wonder what they mean, and whether picking the wrong one will waste your time.

Here's the reassuring part first. Both are well established, both can help with many of the same struggles, and many providers draw on both. This guide is here to help you understand the difference, not to make you choose perfectly.

## CBT, in a sentence

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) looks at the links between what you think, how you feel and what you do, and helps you change the patterns that keep you stuck.

In practice, CBT tends to be structured and focused on the present. You and your provider agree on goals, notice the thoughts that show up in hard moments, and test whether they hold up. You'll often practice skills between sessions, like catching a worry spiral early, or slowly facing something you've been avoiding.

CBT is often shorter-term, and it has a large body of research behind it for anxiety, depression, panic, insomnia and more.

## Psychodynamic therapy, in a sentence

Psychodynamic therapy explores how earlier experiences, and feelings you may not be fully aware of, shape the way you feel, relate and react today.

Sessions are usually more open. Instead of an agenda, you talk about what's on your mind, and your provider helps you notice themes: patterns in relationships, feelings that keep coming back, the ways the past echoes in the present. The relationship with your provider can itself become part of the work.

It can be shorter or longer term, and its research base has grown a great deal, especially for depression and for long-standing emotional and relationship patterns.

## What a session might feel like

A CBT session might begin with how your week went against the goals you set, move into one situation that was hard, and end with something to try before next time. It can feel a little like working with a coach.

A psychodynamic session might begin wherever you are that day. Your provider might point out that you've mentioned your father three times, or that you apologize whenever you disagree. It can feel more like a slow, guided conversation with yourself.

Neither is better. They're different ways in.

## How to choose

A few honest questions can point you in a direction:

- **Do you want tools for a specific problem right now?** CBT's structure often suits that.
- **Do you keep ending up in the same place, and want to understand why?** A psychodynamic approach may fit.
- **Do you like having something to practice, or would you rather talk?** Both answers are fine.

Then remember the finding that holds across almost every kind of therapy: the relationship between you and your provider matters a great deal, often as much as the method. Feeling heard, respected and understood is a strong sign you're in the right place.

On PsychMind you can filter by approach, so you can look for providers who list CBT, psychodynamic therapy or both. If you're not sure, leave it open and read how providers describe their work in their own words.
