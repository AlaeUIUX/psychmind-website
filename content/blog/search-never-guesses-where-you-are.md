## Three questions, not thirty filters

When we started designing search, we wrote down what people actually say when they ask a friend for a recommendation. It's rarely "a female LMHC trained in CBT and EMDR who takes my insurance." It's closer to: I've been struggling with anxiety, I'd rather talk online, and I think I'd feel more comfortable with a woman.

So the search bar asks three things. How you'd like to meet: online or in person. What's on your mind, in your own words or picked from suggestions like anxiety, trauma or family issues. And who feels right: the kind of person you'd be comfortable with.

Everything else, like approach, language, age group, insurance and price, lives in the filters beside the results for when you want them. You don't have to touch any of them to get a good answer.

## How the filters add up

Behind the bar, the rules are simple enough to explain in two sentences, which was the point.

Inside one filter, any choice counts: pick anxiety and depression, and a provider who works with either will show up. Across filters, everything has to match: anxiety or depression, *and* online, *and* Spanish-speaking.

Next to every option, we show how many providers it would give you with the rest of your choices applied. If ticking a box would leave you with nothing, you can see that before you tick it.

## The one rule that never bends

Most filters can bend a little when results run thin. Location can't.

A provider can only see you in a state where they're licensed, in a room or on a screen. So on PsychMind, online doesn't mean "anywhere." It means a provider licensed in your state. In person means a practice near you: the same ZIP code first, then your city, then the ZIP codes around it, then the rest of your state.

We never quietly widen that circle to fill out a page. A result you can't actually book isn't a result. It's a disappointment that arrives a week later.

## Close matches, labelled honestly

The other filters are different. If a search finds fewer than ten providers, we also show close matches: people who meet everything except one of your other choices. Each one says plainly what it misses, so a provider who doesn't take your insurance is shown as exactly that, not slipped in among the rest.

That's how a good friend recommends someone too. "She doesn't take your insurance, but she's wonderful with anxiety" is useful. Leaving out the first half isn't.

## What we're still working on

We'd rather be honest about the rough edges. In-person search works from ZIP codes, cities and states today, not from distances on a map, so "near you" is a careful estimate rather than a measured one. Better location matching is on our list.

Search also runs only on what providers have told us and what our team has verified. It doesn't learn from what you click, and it doesn't build a picture of you. That's on purpose, and it's the subject of our next post.
